# NY Medicaid: was administrative attrition foreseeable? A veracity check (2026-10-16)

*Operator-directed. The operator pasted an October 9 implementation report on New York's H.R. 1
work-requirement rollout and a second model's answer to the operator's claim. The claim:
"something tells me studies have been showing that to happen many years before this year."
Purpose: test that claim, and test the second model's answer, which opened "Your statement is
highly accurate." That opening is a primed agreement, so it gets a disconfirming test.*

**Provenance:** `[IN-FRAMEWORK]`, compiled by the proposer and **not self-verified**. Each
citation below was checked against a search result or a primary document this session. Where a
check rests only on press coverage or a secondary summary, the row says so.

**Analyst prior, disclosed:** the proposer expected the claim to hold. §4 is aimed against that
expectation.

**Track:** a Track C (foreseeability) question. No Track A act entry exists yet for New York's
implementation, so this is filed as a brief, not as a `TC-###` entry. §6 lists what a Track C
entry would need.

| "CMS explained as early as 2022 that withholding IRS consent does not prevent ex parte renewal" | CMS, *Ex Parte Renewal: Strategies to Maximize Automation…*, **Oct 20, 2022**, slide 19 (verbatim below) | **Verified, and stronger than the second model said** |

CMS, Oct 20 2022, slide 19 (under "States Must Not"):

> "Require consent before applying ex parte processes to beneficiary renewals – this isn't
> permitted. Beneficiary consent is required for to obtain IRS data, and can be requested for up
> to five years. Beneficiary consent is not required for ex parte renewals – even if a
> beneficiary withholds consent for use of IRS data, the state can still conduct ex parte
> reviews with other reliable data sources."

CBPP's unwinding guide says the same: states could not drop a case from ex parte because the
person had not consented to the use of federal tax information.

**Net:** five of the seven citations hold. One is narrower than presented. One could not be
located. Two of the five that hold are dated 2025 and support the mechanism, but not the
"years before" timing. "Highly accurate" was a fair verdict on the claim. The answer was looser
in the evidence it gave for it.

The Arkansas study is the closest precedent for this case, not the IRS-consent mechanism. Same
policy (work requirements), same failure: eligible people lost coverage through the reporting
process.

## 4. Disconfirming test: where the claim could be wrong

1. **The consent question may itself be lawful, or required.** Using federal tax information
   needs the enrollee's consent (CMS 2022, slide 19). Bringing the question back may be a
   compliance correction, not a careless design choice. **This changes where the problem
   sits.** Asking is defensible. What CMS has said states may not do is let a refusal stop the
   ex parte attempt. The second model's line, "the State built a renewal process in which one
   answer can disable a major continuity mechanism," is a fair *hypothesis*. It is not yet
   established. Whether the drop comes from non-consenters being routed out of ex parte, or
   from weak fallback data, has not been disclosed.
2. **"Dropped sharply" has no numbers behind it.** The source is a secondhand summary (NYSAFP,
   Oct 9 2026) of remarks at a roundtable. No rate, denominator, period, or comparison has been
   published. An unmeasured drop cannot carry a magnitude claim.
3. **Lower ex parte rates are not the same as coverage loss.** People who fail ex parte get a
   pre-filled form and can still renew. The coverage effect depends on how many of those forms
   come back. Unwinding data (§2) says many do not, but New York's own figure is unknown.
4. **Hindsight bias.** "Not surprising" is easy to say after the fact. This test is limited to
   evidence dated **before** the 2025 reintroduction. The two 2025 papers are excluded from
   the foreseeability case on that ground.

None of the four defeats 1a or 1b. Points 1–3 bound 1c and 1d.

## 6. What a Track C entry would need

- **A Track A act to attach to.** For example: the 2025 reintroduction of the IRS-consent
  question, *if* records show non-consenters were routed out of ex parte. The classification
  is the operator's call.
- **Constructive foreseeability:** §2–§3. This part is already held.
- **Actual foreseeability:** evidence that the CMS Oct 2022 deck, or the unwinding
  ex parte-compliance findings (Sept 2023), reached NY DOH. CMS ran this as a learning
  collaborative for states, so that evidence is likely to exist. It is not held.
- **Documented awareness:** the State's statement that rates "dropped sharply," and that it is
  rewriting the question, shows awareness *after* the fact. It is not awareness before the
  decision.

## 7. Demands this supports (step one, not step two)

**Disclosure:**
1. Ex parte success rates before and after the 2025 consent question, broken out by consent
   status.
2. Whether non-consenters still receive an ex parte attempt using SWICA/QWD, SNAP, SSA, or
   The Work Number data, as CMS 2022 slide 19 says they must.
3. Procedural-closure counts for non-consenters.
4. CMS approval documents and the county-assignment logic for the Bronx exemption.
5. How failed Truv matches are coded, so that no match is not recorded as noncompliance.

**Inquiry:** whether New York's ex parte process met the CMS consent rule during 2025–2026.

## BOUNDARY

**This establishes:**
- The mechanism (small administrative changes causing coverage loss among eligible people) was
  documented in the literature, and in CMS's own guidance, years before New York reintroduced
  the question in 2025.
- Five of the second model's seven citations hold. Two of those five do not support its timing.
- CMS said in writing, in Oct 2022, that withheld IRS consent must not stop ex parte renewal.

**This does not establish, on its own:**
- That New York actually routes non-consenters out of ex parte.
- That any New Yorker has lost coverage from the consent question.
- The size of the drop.
- That any official acted with knowledge of the harm.
- That New York's process breaks federal rules. That is a question to put to the State, not a
  finding.

One reported result in one state is an instance, not a pattern. The pattern claim rests on the
multi-state literature in §2–§3, not on New York.
