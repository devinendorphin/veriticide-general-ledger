Coefficient Giving’s decision to scale its philanthropic commitments to $1 billion in 2026 represents a watershed moment for what began as a modest collaboration between tech wealth and charity evaluation. The San Francisco organization — formerly known as Open Philanthropy and funded by Good Ventures, the foundation of Facebook co-founder Dustin Moskovitz and Cari Tuna — has directed a total of about $7 billion since 2014. The $1 billion 2026 commitment reflects a decision to dramatically increase allocations to GiveWell’s recommended global health groups, representing a nearly sixfold jump over prior levels. Critically, critics and analysts argue that while the global health giving is genuine, it is the organization’s AI safety and regulation spending that raises the most profound questions about the role of concentrated private wealth in shaping the governance of a transformative technology. AI safety will be harder given the billions of dollars at play, but Coefficient is undaunted — it directed more than $100 million to AI safety initiatives in a recent period.
The controversy sharpened dramatically in recent days following the public resignation of Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon, whose post on September 9, 2026 quickly became the centerpiece of a debate about manufactured narratives and coordinated influence campaigns. Coxon resigned as an Anthropic researcher, stating the company and its competitors were not acting responsibly and claiming there was a 10% chance AI would cause human extinction within the next 10 years. Policy analyst Parker Thayer publicly challenged the organic nature of that moment. According to Thayer, the first three quote-posts arrived within 15 minutes — two within 10 — coming from Nathan Calvin, general counsel of Encode AI; Peter Wildeford of the AI Policy Network; and Daniel Kokotajlo, who leads the AI Futures Project. The 14th quote-poster, about 27 minutes in, worked on AI safety at Coefficient Giving. Coxon’s account had almost no prior activity, yet the post drew 165 million views. Adding another layer of financial entanglement, in 2022, Jacob Coxon had received a $20,000 scholarship from the Good Ventures Foundation’s “Long-Term Future Scholarship Program.”
Beyond the Coxon episode, scrutiny of Coefficient Giving’s funding of AI governance bodies long predates this latest flare-up. Coefficient Giving supports AI work through its Navigating Transformative AI fund, which seeks to reduce the risk of AI-caused global catastrophes. The organization has been supporting AI safety and security work since 2015, including work to make AI systems more trustworthy and controllable, as well as AI governance and policy advocacy. To date, Coefficient Giving has made more than 440 grants through its Navigating Transformative AI fund. Prior reporting revealed that the organization, under its former Open Philanthropy name, was a primary funder of the Center for Security and Emerging Technology (CSET) at Georgetown University — a group that placed fellows in key government offices during the Biden administration era, including roles in the White House’s National Security Council and the Department of Commerce, with those fellows’ salaries paid through CSET.
📚 Background & Context
In 2011, when Cari Tuna and Dustin Moskovitz were launching their foundation Good Ventures, they met with representatives from GiveWell to discuss their philanthropy, leading to the creation of GiveWell Labs — a collaboration that explored what an affluent donor should fund to have the greatest impact. Three years later, GiveWell Labs was rebranded as the Open Philanthropy Project. Tuna and Moskovitz were inspired by Peter Singer’s The Life You Can Save and became the youngest couple to sign Bill Gates and Warren Buffett’s Giving Pledge, promising to give away most of their money. The effective altruism movement’s most infamous brush with big tech philanthropy, however, went down in flames when Sam Bankman-Fried’s flagrant mismanagement and fraud burned EA-aligned nonprofits that had banked on promised grants from the FTX Foundation’s giving vehicle.
Central to critics’ concerns is what they describe as a self-reinforcing loop: Moskovitz funds AI companies while simultaneously funding the advocacy groups that call for strict regulation of those same companies — regulation that could, in theory, entrench well-capitalized incumbents like Anthropic by raising the compliance barrier for competitors. As critics have noted, the people who own Anthropic also fund the movement demanding that Anthropic be regulated, with Moskovitz having more than $1 billion out the door in AI safety advocacy and another $1 billion committed for the current year. Moskovitz was an early backer of Anthropic, which shares a web of institutional, financial, and personal connections with Coefficient Giving. Holden Karnofsky co-founded Open Philanthropy, later joined Anthropic to work on responsible-scaling policy and safety planning, and is married to Anthropic president Daniela Amodei. Defenders of the network argue these connections reflect a coherent philosophical commitment rather than strategic market manipulation, noting that a grant is not a command, an investment is not operational control, a marriage is not evidence of wrongdoing, and shared ideology is not proof of a common script.
The broader regulatory landscape presents competing frameworks. The White House’s March 2026 AI legislative framework called for streamlined permitting, removal of barriers to deployment, and a uniform national framework, while a June executive order said the administration would promote AI innovation and security while deploying advanced systems rapidly. That posture stands in direct tension with the AI safety movement’s calls for moratoria and strict oversight mechanisms. Anthropologist Mollie Gleiberman, who has studied effective altruism, has noted that the movement has helped push topics like digital sentience and the risk of human extinction from AI further into the mainstream. Whether that mainstreaming reflects genuine public risk awareness or philanthropically manufactured urgency is now the defining question for policymakers, journalists, and the public alike. As Coefficient Giving’s funding commitments grow and more Anthropic-adjacent wealth potentially enters the philanthropic ecosystem, the pressure on legislators to distinguish independent expert consensus from funded advocacy is only intensifying.
