# Housing First vs Treatment First: the evidence base, with limitations on both sides (2026-10-03)

*Operator-directed. Companion to `cicero-homelessness-map-2026-10-03.md`. Purpose: assemble
the evidence that makes Housing First "evidence-based," set it against the evidence for
treatment first, and be equally rigorous about the limits of each.*

**Provenance:** `[IN-FRAMEWORK]`, compiled by the proposer and **not self-verified** (Priority
32). **Analyst prior, disclosed:** the proposer came into this leaning toward Housing First.
The audit in §6 is designed against that lean, and the file should go to a non-proposer
reviewer.

**Grades used here:**
- **RCT**: randomized controlled trial;
- **SR**: systematic review or meta-analysis;
- **OBS**: observational, system-level, or before-and-after;
- **MOD**: simulation model;
- **U**: not found in this search. That means not yet evidenced either way. It does not mean
  absent.

---

## 0. Definitions: three different things get called "treatment first"

| Label | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Housing First (HF)** | Immediate permanent housing with **no preconditions** (no required sobriety or treatment), voluntary support services, harm reduction, and client choice. It is both a *program model* (Pathways to Housing, 1992) and a *system policy* (HUD funding preference from 2013) | Pathways; At Home/Chez Soi; HUD-VASH; Houston; Finland |
| **TF-1: treatment first as a program** | The "linear" or "staircase" continuum: sobriety and treatment are **preconditions** for moving up to permanent housing | The continuum-of-care comparison arms in HF trials |
| **TF-2: contingency management** | Housing *is provided*, but **continued access depends on abstinence**, verified by testing | Milby et al. (Birmingham) |
| **TF-3: the enforcement and coercion package** | Camping bans, encampment sweeps, funding tied to enforcement, **involuntary commitment** | Cicero model bill; EO 14321 |

**The policy debate routinely collapses these.** The evidence for TF-2 is not evidence for
TF-1 or TF-3. The Cicero / EO 14321 package (TF-3) borrows the language of "treatment," but
its distinctive parts are enforcement and coercion.

---

## 1. Housing First: the evidence base

| Study | Design | Finding | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tsemberis, Gulcur & Nakae 2004** (*Am J Public Health*), New York City, people with both mental illness and addiction | RCT, n=225: HF vs housing conditional on treatment and sobriety | **About 80%** stably housed at 12 months vs **24%** for continuum programs; about 80% vs 30% of time housed at 24 months. **No worsening** of psychiatric or substance-use symptoms | RCT |
| **At Home/Chez Soi** (Canada; 5 cities, 2009–13) | RCT, n≈2,000+: HF vs treatment as usual | HF with intensive community treatment teams: **+45.8 percentage points** time stably housed. Some gains in community functioning, some quality-of-life subscales, and arrests at some time points. **No difference** in substance use, health-service use, or community integration. **69% of costs offset** by savings elsewhere; net cost about CA$6,311 per person per year | RCT |
| **Baxter et al. 2019** (*J Epidemiol Community Health*) | SR and meta-analysis of RCTs | HF **improves housing stability**. **No clear difference in substance use** (no increase). May improve some health aspects and reduce emergency service use | SR |
| **National Academies 2018**, *Permanent Supportive Housing* | Expert consensus review | Supportive housing **improves housing stability**. Evidence of **health improvement exists only for HIV/AIDS**; evidence for other health conditions is **lacking**, mainly because of research limits | SR (consensus) |
| **HUD-VASH, veterans** | OBS (national counts) | Veteran homelessness **fell about 55.6%, 2010–2024**, credited to VASH vouchers plus services | OBS |
| **Houston** (city system) | OBS | Homelessness cut about **63%** in a decade; more than 25,000 people housed directly, with no sobriety precondition | OBS |
| **Finland** (national) | OBS | Long-term homelessness **fell 68%, 2008–2022**; it has risen again in recent years | OBS |

**What makes HF "evidence-based":** two or more independent RCTs plus a meta-analysis show a
large, durable effect **on housing stability**, the outcome the model targets, without
worsening substance use. System-level adopters show large falls in the populations they
targeted.

## 2. Housing First: limitations

1. **The strong evidence is about one outcome.** Housing stability is robust. Substance use,
   mental-health symptoms, and overall health show **no consistent improvement** (Chez Soi;
   Baxter; NASEM). HF advocates who claim health or recovery gains **overclaim**.
2. **Cost savings are partial, not net-positive.** Chez Soi offset 69%; HF **costs more**
   than it saves. The claim that HF "pays for itself" does not hold at these grades.
3. **Narrow trial population.** Mostly chronically homeless adults with serious mental
   illness. The evidence for people with **active, severe addiction** was thin enough that
   Kertesz et al. (2009, *Milbank Quarterly*) warned against generalizing.
4. **Weak comparators.** Several trials compare HF against *treatment as usual*, not against a
   well-resourced treatment-first program. HF beats the status quo. It has fewer tests against
   strong alternatives.
5. **Developer allegiance.** The foundational trial was run by the model's originator
   (Tsemberis). Chez Soi is independent and replicates the housing result, which mitigates
   this.
6. **Fidelity varies.** "Housing First" as funded policy often lacks the intensive services
   the trials used. Effects from fidelity programs may not carry over to diluted versions.
7. **HF does not create housing.** It places people into existing units. **The system-level
   picture after the 2013 HUD adoption is the critics' strongest card:** overall homelessness
   **rose about 27%** and chronic homelessness **about 81%** since 2013 (current HUD
   leadership's figures).
8. **OBS successes are confounded.** Houston, VASH, and Finland coincided with **large new
   funding and housing supply**. HF's specific contribution cannot be separated from the money
   and the units.

## 3. Treatment first: the evidence base

| Study | Design | Finding | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| **TF-1 arms in HF trials** (Tsemberis 2004, and others) | RCT, as the comparison arm | **24–30% housing retention** vs about 80% for HF. **No better** symptom outcomes | RCT (*against* TF-1 on housing) |
| **Milby et al. 2005** (*Am J Public Health*), cocaine-dependent homeless adults | RCT, n=196: no housing vs **abstinence-contingent housing** vs non-contingent housing, all with day treatment | **Abstinence:** contingent housing > non-contingent housing > no housing. **All three groups improved** on employment and housing | RCT (**TF-2**) |
| **Kertesz et al. 2009** (*Milbank Quarterly*) | Narrative review | HF's evidence for **active addiction** is limited, and recovery-oriented housing has evidence for abstinence. **Caution, not refutation** | Review |
| **Compulsory community treatment**: Kisely et al., Cochrane | SR of RCTs | **No clear difference** in service use, social functioning, or quality of life vs voluntary care. People under compulsion were **less likely to be crime victims**; it is unclear whether compulsion or intensity explains that. **Three small trials, low-to-moderate quality** | SR (**TF-3 coercion**) |
| **Encampment sweeps and camping bans**: Barocas et al. 2023 (*JAMA*) | MOD, using data from 23 US cities | Involuntary displacement projected to contribute to **15–25% of deaths** among unhoused people who inject drugs over 10 years, plus more overdoses and hospitalizations | MOD (**TF-3 enforcement**: a harm signal) |
| **Camping bans reducing homelessness** | — | **No RCT or strong quasi-experimental evidence found in this search** | **U** |
| **Recovery housing** (e.g. Oxford House) as a system alternative | — | Not searched this session | **U** |

**What the case for treatment first rests on:**
- **TF-2 (contingency management) has real RCT support for abstinence.** Contingency
  management has a strong evidence base in addiction treatment generally.
- The critique of HF's narrow outcomes and its weak record with active addiction is **valid**
  (§2.1, §2.3).
- **The post-2013 rise in aggregate homelessness**, which is the main argument used for
  EO 14321.

## 4. Treatment first: limitations

1. **TF-1 loses its head-to-head tests on housing.** Where linear treatment first was
   randomized against HF, it retained a third or less as many people in housing.
2. **The best treatment-first trial is not a treatment-first-as-precondition trial.** In
   Milby, the contingent group **was given housing** from the start. Its finding supports
   *conditioning continued housing on abstinence within a housing-provided model*. It does
   not support withholding housing until treatment. Even **non-contingent housing beat no
   housing.** The study is also small, single-site, and limited to cocaine dependence.
   Whether abstinence-contingent evictions left people worse off is **U**.
3. **Coercion's evidence is weak.** The Cochrane review finds no clear benefit on core
   outcomes, from small, low-quality trials. EO 14321's expansion of commitment is **not
   supported** by that base. It is not refuted either; the evidence is thin.
4. **Enforcement's evidence leans toward harm.** The best available estimate is a simulation
   showing excess mortality. No strong evidence was found that bans reduce homelessness.
   Simulations depend on their assumptions, so this is a signal, not a measured effect.
5. **The aggregate argument is correlational.** The rise since 2013 coincides with HF
   adoption, but also with rising rents and falling vacancies. Colburn & Aldern (2022), using
   point-in-time counts from 2007–2019, find that **rents and vacancy rates explain regional
   variation in homelessness**, while drug use, mental illness, and poverty do not; individual
   factors act as moderators. The post-2013 rise does not show that HF caused it any more than
   Houston's fall shows HF alone caused that.
6. **Fewer independent trials overall**, and advocacy-sourced claims (think tanks, the
   administration) supply much of the public case.

## 5. Head-to-head by outcome

| Outcome | HF | TF-1 (precondition) | TF-2 (contingency) | TF-3 (enforcement and coercion) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Housing stability** | **Strong (RCT, SR)** | **Weak; loses RCTs** | All arms improved (RCT, small) | **U**; harm signal from sweeps (MOD) |
| **Substance use** | No change, no worsening (RCT, SR) | No advantage shown | **Better abstinence (RCT, small)** | U |
| **Mental health** | No consistent improvement | No advantage shown | U | No clear difference (Cochrane, low quality) |
| **Mortality** | U | U | U | **Excess deaths projected (MOD)** |
| **Cost** | Partial offset; net cost (RCT) | U | U | U |
| **Aggregate homelessness** | OBS gains where funded; national rise since 2013 (confounded) | U | U | **U**: no strong evidence |

## 6. Reading, with symmetric discipline

- **HF is evidence-based for what it targets: getting and keeping people housed.** That claim
  is strong. Claims that it improves health, sobriety, or cost are **not** supported at the
  same strength, and HF advocates who make them overclaim.
- **The strongest pro-treatment evidence (contingency management) is compatible with
  providing housing.** It argues for *adding* contingent supports inside housing, not for
  withholding housing.
- **The Cicero / EO 14321 package rests on its weakest legs.** Enforcement has a harm signal.
  Coercion shows no clear benefit. The aggregate-trend argument is confounded. This is the
  same step-one standard the ledger applies everywhere: it is a **basis to demand the
  evidence**, not a finding of bad faith.
- **Both camps underweight housing supply.** The structural evidence (Colburn & Aldern)
  suggests neither program model can offset rents and vacancy rates. Program debates can
  displace the supply question.
- **Open questions (U):**
  - recovery-housing outcomes;
  - whether camping bans reduce homelessness;
  - mortality under HF;
  - HF results for active severe addiction at scale;
  - TF-2 results beyond cocaine dependence.

## 7. Bearing on TD-009 and the Cicero map

- **The evidence does not favor conditioning support on psychiatric treatment as a route to
  housing stability.** That is the structure her family imposed, in her account, and the one
  EO 14321 adopts. The supported route is unconditional housing plus *voluntary* services.
  The contingency evidence concerns **abstinence from drugs**, not compliance with psychiatric
  medication.
- This is an evidence statement about policy models, not a clinical judgment about her.

## BOUNDARY

**Establishes:**
- the evidence each model has, by outcome, at the stated grades;
- that "treatment first" covers three distinct models with different evidence;
- the limitations of both bases.

**Does NOT establish:**
- the right policy (values and supply questions sit outside this ledger);
- that any actor argues in bad faith;
- clinical guidance for any individual;
- anything marked **U**.

# Institutionalization and coercive treatment: history, abuses, revival (2026-10-03)

*Operator-directed. Context for TD-009, the Cicero map, and the evidence comparison. The
question has three parts:*
1. *how institutionalization developed in the West and in the US;*
2. *how coercive treatment has been used to silence dissent or override autonomy in
   vulnerable populations, and what people experienced inside, including the system's
   corruptions;*
3. *what is now being done to revive it.*

**Provenance:** `[IN-FRAMEWORK]`, compiled by the proposer and not self-verified (Priority
32).

**Grades:** **P1** (court rulings, settlements, statutes) · **S1** (major press or scholarly
works) · **S2** (general reference). Items marked **(gen.)** are drawn from general historical
knowledge and were **not re-verified in this session's searches**.

**Discipline:**
- **The serious-illness counter-case is recorded, not suppressed.** Some people do suffer
  terribly without care, and the revival responds partly to them.
- **Misuse of coercion does not show that all coercion is misuse.** This file documents how
  the power gets used against people, and makes no claim beyond that.

---

## 1. How institutionalization developed

| Period | Development | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Medieval to 18th c. | Bethlem ("Bedlam"), London: custodial confinement. Foucault's "Great Confinement" thesis (the Paris Hôpital Général, 1656) frames mass confinement of the poor, idle, and mad as social control. **Historians such as Porter and Midelfort dispute its scope and timing.** It is used here as a lens, not as settled fact | (gen.) |
| 1790s–1840s | **"Moral treatment"** (Tuke's York Retreat, 1796; Pinel in Paris): humane reform. In the US, **Dorothea Dix** campaigned for state asylums, and they spread rapidly | (gen.) |
| 1851 | **"Drapetomania"** (Samuel Cartwright): a "disease" invented to explain enslaved people fleeing. **The pathologizing of resistance is present at the founding of American psychiatric classification** | (gen.) |
| 1860–64 | **Elizabeth Packard** (§2) | S1 |
| 1880s–1920s | Asylums grow into custodial warehouses. **Eugenics**: *Buck v. Bell* (1927, 8–1) upheld forced sterilization (§2) | P1 |
| 1930s–50s | Somatic treatments: insulin coma, electroconvulsive therapy without consent, **lobotomy** (Freeman and Watts) | S1 / (gen.) |
| **1955** | **Peak: about 560,000** people in US state psychiatric hospitals | S1 |
| 1954–1980s | **Deinstitutionalization**, driven by several forces: | S1 / P1 |
| | • chlorpromazine (1954); | (gen.) |
| | • JFK's Community Mental Health Act (1963); | (gen.) |
| | • Medicaid's exclusion of state psychiatric hospitals (1965), which shifted costs onto states; | (gen.) |
| | • the civil-rights litigation of the 1970s (§4); | P1 |
| | • the 1981 repeal of the Mental Health Systems Act, which removed much of the promised community care | (gen.) |
| Today | Fewer than **45,000** state psychiatric beds, **a 95% per-capita decline**. Jails and prisons have become the de facto psychiatric system: mental illness affects **64% of jail inmates and 56% of state prisoners** (as cited by Sisti et al.). This is **"trans-institutionalization"**: people moved from hospitals to jails, nursing homes, and the street | S1 |

**Load-bearing point.** Deinstitutionalization **closed the asylums, but never built the
community system that was meant to replace them.** Both camps in today's debate accept this
fact. They draw opposite conclusions from it.

---

## 2. Coercive treatment used against dissent and autonomy

| Case | What happened | Mechanism | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Elizabeth Packard** (Illinois, 1860–64) | Committed by her husband, a minister, after **religious disagreement**. Illinois law required a hearing to commit a man, but **a married woman could be committed at her husband's request, without evidence**. She was held three years. **A jury found her sane in seven minutes (1864).** Her campaign led to commitment-reform laws and a legislative investigation that **confirmed her account of asylum conditions** | **A family member's power to commit, used against a dissenting woman** | S1 |
| **Carrie Buck** (*Buck v. Bell*, 1927) | Institutionalized after becoming pregnant as a teenager. **She said she had been raped by her foster family's nephew.** The family called her promiscuous. The Supreme Court upheld her sterilization ("Three generations of imbeciles are enough"). Later research found she was **of ordinary intelligence and the victim of rape** | **Institutionalization used to remove a rape victim and protect her abuser's family**; the testifier pathologized | P1 / S1 |
| **Rosemary Kennedy** (1941) | Her father, Joseph Kennedy Sr., **arranged a lobotomy at 23 without consulting the family**. Freeman and Watts operated while she was awake. She lost coherent speech and mobility and lived **the rest of her life institutionalized**. Her brother's 1963 Community Mental Health Act is often linked to her | **A powerful family patriarch controlling a daughter's mind and body** | S1 |
| **"Protest psychosis"** (Metzl, 2009; Ionia State Hospital, Michigan) | From the 1960s, Black men, **some committed after civil-rights demonstrations**, were diagnosed with schizophrenia en masse. The diagnosis shifted from "docile white women" to "hostile Black men." **"Some patients became schizophrenic because of changes in their diagnosis rather than their clinical symptoms."** Black men are still diagnosed with schizophrenia at disproportionate rates | **Political protest pathologized; race-coded diagnosis** | S1 |
| **Homosexuality** | Listed as a mental disorder until **1973**. Aversion "therapies" and commitment were used against gay people | **Identity pathologized** | (gen.) |
| **Military whistleblowers** | Commanders' **retaliatory referrals for mental-health evaluations** were common enough that Congress acted: the **FY1993 defense authorization act, §546**, made such a referral a reviewable personnel action. **DoD Directive 6490.1** (now DoDI 6490.04) **prohibits using a mental-health evaluation as reprisal** | **Psychiatric referral as reprisal, recognized and banned by statute** | P1 |
| **Adrian Schoolcraft** (NYPD, 2009) | After secretly recording **arrest quotas and manipulated crime statistics**, he was forcibly taken from his apartment by a group of officers led by a deputy chief and **held six days at Jamaica Hospital, partly in the psychiatric ward.** His recordings later became "smoking gun" evidence in the stop-and-frisk case (*Floyd*). Settlements: about **$600K from the NYPD** (2015), plus a separate settlement with the hospital | **Involuntary psychiatric hold used against a whistleblower** | S1 / P1 |
| **Britney Spears conservatorship** (2008–2021) | Under a conservatorship led by her father for 13 years, she testified (23 Jun 2021) to being **medicated against her will (lithium), forced to work, kept from her earnings, and prevented from removing a contraceptive device.** She compared her father to a "sex trafficker" who "loved" the control. **Terminated 12 Nov 2021**, with no opposition | **A family-controlled legal guardianship joining psychiatric framing, medication, and control of money** | S1 / P1 |
| **Comparator: Soviet "punitive psychiatry"** | Dissidents diagnosed with "sluggish schizophrenia" and held in psychiatric hospitals | The Western reference case, often cited abroad and rarely applied at home | (gen.) |

**Pattern across the cases** (a tendency claim; the cases were selected for fit):
- **Who is targeted:** wives, daughters, rape survivors, Black protesters, gay people,
  whistleblowers. People whose speech or autonomy **inconveniences someone with power over
  them.**
- **Who holds the lever:** often **family** (Packard's husband, Kennedy's father, Spears's
  father) or **the institution being exposed** (the NYPD, military commands).
- **The move:** a label of mental illness **turns dissent into a symptom**, so the
  institution never has to answer the dissent itself. This is the ledger's **Move 3**
  (disqualification) **with physical custody attached.**

---

## 3. What people experienced inside: corruptions of the system

| Corruption | Evidence | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| **Squalor and neglect** | **Willowbrook State School** (Staten Island): overcrowding, filth, and abuse were exposed by television reporting in 1972. Litigation ended in the **1975 consent judgment**, *NYSARC v. Carey*, which set minimum standards of care | P1 / S1 |
| **Unpaid forced labor ("institutional peonage")** | Patients and residents ran farms, laundries, and kitchens for no pay, sometimes labeled as "therapy." ***Souder v. Brennan*** **(1973)** held that federal wage law covers patient-workers | P1 |
| **Custody without treatment** | ***O'Connor v. Donaldson*** **(1975):** Kenneth Donaldson was held **almost 15 years** in a Florida state hospital. He was not dangerous and **received no treatment**. The Supreme Court held that a state cannot confine a non-dangerous person who can survive in freedom | P1 |
| **Deaths in restraint and seclusion** | ***Hartford Courant*, "Deadly Restraint" (1998):** at least **142 deaths in a decade**, with an estimated **50–150 a year** nationally (Harvard Center for Risk Analysis) | S1 |
| **Surgery that could not be undone** | Lobotomies (Kennedy); forced sterilization (Buck) | S1 / P1 |
| **Detention for profit (current)** | **Universal Health Services** paid **$122M (2020)** to resolve False Claims Act allegations that it admitted patients who did not need hospitalization, **kept patients longer than necessary**, chemically restrained them, and paid illegal inducements (2006–2018) | P1 (settlement; allegations resolved without admission of liability) |
| | **Acadia Healthcare** (*New York Times*, Sept 2024): based on more than 50 former and current staff, it **held patients against their will for financial reasons**, exaggerated symptoms, and adjusted medication to justify longer stays (about $2,200 a day). Complaints came from at least 12 of its 19 states. **Federal scrutiny** followed, including the VA inspector general, Manhattan prosecutors, and a Missouri grand jury | S1 (investigation; not adjudicated) |
| **A flagged cautionary source** | **Rosenhan, "On Being Sane in Insane Places" (1973)**, the classic study of misdiagnosed sane people. Cahalan (*The Great Pretender*, 2019) found **evidence that Rosenhan misrepresented and probably fabricated** parts of the data. **It is not relied on here.** It is recorded because citing it uncritically would weaken the case, and the case stands without it | S1 |

**The structural point about corruption.** A system that **detains people on a label and
gets paid per day of detention** creates an incentive to keep people in. UHS and Acadia are
the modern form of that incentive. The Hartford Courant deaths and *Donaldson* are what
custody looks like without external checks. The ledger's general thesis applies: **harm
tracks the absence of an independent check.**

## 4. The legal pushback that deinstitutionalization produced (P1)

- ***Lessard v. Schmidt*** **(1972):** procedural due process for civil commitment. (gen.)
- ***O'Connor v. Donaldson*** **(1975):** a non-dangerous person cannot be confined.
- **Willowbrook consent judgment (1975).**
- ***Olmstead v. L.C.*** **(1999):** under the Americans with Disabilities Act, unnecessary
  institutionalization is discrimination; services must be in the most integrated setting.
  (gen.)
- **International:** the **UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities** (Arts.
  12 and 14) is read by its committee as **prohibiting detention based on disability**. The
  US has **not ratified** it. (gen.)

## 5. The revival: what is being done now

| Measure | Content | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| **NYC directive** (Nov 2022) | Police and outreach teams may **involuntarily remove** people judged unable to meet "basic human needs," **as judged in the moment** | S1 |
| **New York State budget** (May 2025) | **Lowers the commitment standard**: inability to meet basic needs now counts as danger to self. Advocates condemned it; lawmakers had rejected earlier versions | S1 |
| **California SB 43** (signed Oct 2023; phased in 2024–26) | The first major revision of the state's commitment law in over 50 years. **Expands "grave disability"** to include severe **substance use disorder**, which **widens conservatorship** | P1 |
| **EO 14321** (24 Jul 2025) | Federal push for **expanded civil commitment**, encampment removal, and treatment-conditioned funding (see the Cicero map) | P1 |
| **The intellectual case** | **Sisti, Segal & Emanuel, "Bring Back the Asylum"** (*JAMA*, 2015): restore long-term psychiatric care in the original sense of asylum, a refuge, against trans-institutionalization into jails and streets | S1 |

**The counter-case, stated at full strength.**
- Some people with severe psychosis **cannot recognize their illness** (anosognosia), and are
  dying on streets or cycling through jails.
- Families often **cannot get care** for a loved one until a crisis or an arrest.
- The bed shortage is real.
- Sisti et al. argue for a *humane* asylum, not a return to Willowbrook.

These are serious arguments, and some reform advocates and families of the severely ill make
them sincerely.

**Why history weighs on the revival, at step one.**
1. **The safeguards are being lowered, not raised.** The 2022–2025 measures **loosen the
   standard** for detention (from "dangerous" toward "unable to meet basic needs," and now
   substance use). They do not add independent checks.
2. **The evidence for coercion is thin.** The Cochrane review finds **no clear benefit** from
   compulsory community treatment on core outcomes (see the evidence comparison).
3. **The for-profit incentive is live, not historical.** UHS (2020) and Acadia (2024) show
   **detention for money** happening now, in the sector that would receive expanded
   commitments.
4. **"Basic needs" overlaps with poverty.** A standard built on inability to meet basic needs,
   applied to unhoused people, **risks treating destitution as a symptom.** That echoes the
   Great Confinement's mixing of the poor and the mad.
5. **The levers have historically gone to those whom the dissent inconveniences**: family,
   police, commands. Whether the new regimes add **independent review** sufficient to stop
   another Packard, Schoolcraft, or Spears is **U**. **That is the demand at step one:**
   preservation, disclosure, and audit of commitment decisions under the new standards.

---

## 6. Bearing on TD-009 (a structural rhyme, not equivalence)

**The family lever, across 160 years:**
- **Packard:** a husband commits his wife;
- **Kennedy:** a father orders surgery on his daughter;
- **Spears:** a father's conservatorship medicates her and holds her money;
- **Altman (her account):** a family **releases the father's money only if she takes
  psychiatric medication**, and **publicly frames her as mentally ill** when she testifies
  against a powerful sibling.

**The differences of degree are large and recorded.** Annie Altman was **not committed**. No
court order is involved, and her claim about the condition on her inheritance is **her
account**. The rhyme is in **the structure**: psychiatric framing, control of money, and
family power, aimed at a woman whose testimony inconveniences the powerful. With EO 14321 and
the state measures, that framing now also has **an expanding public counterpart**.

## BOUNDARY

**Establishes:**
- the documented history and current measures at the stated grades;
- a recurring pattern (a tendency, from selected cases) of coercive treatment used against
  people whose dissent or autonomy inconvenienced those with power over them;
- documented profit-driven detention, current and settled or investigated;
- that the current revival lowers detention standards.

**Does NOT establish:**
- that all coercive treatment is abuse;
- that the current measures are made in bad faith;
- that any individual's commitment under them would be wrongful;
- anything about Annie Altman beyond her account and the structural rhyme;
- the (gen.) items, which await re-verification.

# Sexual abuse of institutionalized people, and why perpetrators are rarely held accountable (2026-10-03)

**Discipline:**
- **Minors (corrected 2026-10-03, operator challenge, conceded flat).** The first draft used
  the minors rule to *shrink* the record of harm to children. That made injustice to minors
  invisible, which is the opposite of the rule's purpose. **The rule protects children's
  identities, not their abusers or institutions.** Harm to children is documented in full:
  - scale;
  - allegations;
  - regulatory findings;
  - charges, and the absence of charges;
  - institutional and official conduct.

  Children are never named, and no individual child's sexual or health detail is given
  beyond what the official record states at the level of the allegation.
- Allegations are recorded as allegations.
- **Abuse runs in several directions**, and the data shows it; this file records all of them.

**What the children's record adds:**
- **The accountability gap is worse for children.** They are less able to report, less
  believed, often placed by the state (so the placing agency also has something to hide), and
  frequently disabled.
- **The state was a party to concealment** in at least one documented allegation (Chicago
  Lakeshore).
- **Charges reach a small fraction of victims** (Devereux: 11 children charged against 41 or
  more identified, plus 13 later).
- **Facilities exit without a reckoning** (Lakeshore closed "for COVID").

**What the scale data shows:**
- Sexual harm in these settings is **routine, not exceptional**.
- **Referral to police is in the low single digits** wherever it has been measured: under 5%
  in New York (2009), under 5% in the NHS (2019–23), and 2.5% charged in New York (2015).

## 2. Why perpetrators escape accountability: the mechanisms

| # | Mechanism | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The diagnosis disqualifies the witness.** A psychiatric label, or a cognitive or communication disability, lets institutions treat a report as a **symptom** (delusion, sexualized behavior, confusion) rather than **evidence**. This is the ledger's **Move 3** at institutional scale, and the **reversed causal arrow** of TD-009: distress caused by abuse is read as proof that the report is unreliable. Philosopher Miranda Fricker's term for this is **"testimonial injustice"** (gen.) | Implied by the referral rates; explicit in the Sonoma failure to collect evidence |
| 2 | **The institution investigates itself.** In-house police (California's Office of Protective Services) and internal reviews (New York before 2013) | CIR 2012; Hakim 2011 |
| 3 | **Evidence is never collected.** No rape examinations, no forensic interviews. Without evidence, later prosecution is impossible | Sonoma: zero rape examinations across 12 or more reports |
| 4 | **Non-referral despite legal duty.** Mandatory reporting laws exist but are not enforced | Under 5% (NY, NHS) |
| 5 | **Transfer, not termination.** Abusers are moved to other units or facilities, and no cross-employer registry follows them | Hakim 2011 |
| 6 | **Secrecy written into law.** Statutory exemptions from transparency; outcomes hidden even from victims and families | Illinois (Tribune 2016) |
| 7 | **The reformed body under-charges too.** Oversight agencies created in response still bring very few prosecutions | NY Justice Center: 2.5% |
| 8 | **Custody gives power over the complainant.** Staff control phones, visits, records, medication, restraint, and **release dates**. Complaining can bring sedation, restraint, or a longer hold | (gen.) The profit motive for longer holds is documented (UHS 2020; Acadia 2024) |
| 9 | **Money resolves cases, not courts.** Settlements without admission, and False Claims Act payments **to the government** rather than to victims | UHS $122M (2020) |
| 10 | **Understaffing.** Fewer eyes, agency staff with weaker vetting, and less supervision | BuzzFeed UHS reporting; the UK agency-nurse case |

**Synthesis.** Each mechanism separates **the person harmed** from **an independent check**.
Self-investigation, non-referral, secrecy statutes, and custody all keep the complaint inside
the institution that is accused. Where an external check existed (journalism, federal
inspectors, litigation), the harm became visible. That is the ledger's general thesis: **harm
tracks the absence of an independent check.**

## 3. Symmetry: what this file does not hide

- **Staff are victims too.** The NHS data records **20,928 incidents of patients sexually
  abusing staff** over five years. Both directions are real harm. Neither cancels the other.
  Staff victims also face non-referral.
- **Patient-on-patient abuse is the largest patient-victim category** in the NHS data
  (7,464). The failure there is the institution's failure to **protect**, not only abuse by
  staff.
- **Report counts are not proven incidents.** Some reports will be unfounded. But
  **referral rates under 5% mean the system rarely finds out which reports are true.** The
  gap in accountability is a gap in *testing*, and it hides false and true reports alike.
- **Reforms exist:** New York's Justice Center, the Stop Institutional Child Abuse Act, the
  UK sexual-safety collaborative, and Illinois's reforms after the Tribune series. **Their
  effect is partly measured.** The Justice Center's 2.5% charge rate is a warning that a new
  oversight body is not automatically an independent check.

## 4. Bearing on the revival and on TD-009

- **The revival enlarges the population exposed to these mechanisms.** The 2022–25 measures
  (New York, California SB 43, EO 14321) lower the bar for detention and commitment. The
  facilities that would receive people include a for-profit sector **documented** for
  detention-for-profit (UHS, Acadia). **The accountability gap above is not being closed
  alongside the expansion.** No new independent checks were found in the measures reviewed
  (**U**: not exhaustively searched). **Step-one demand:** before expansion, measure and
  publish rates of sexual abuse, police referral, and prosecution in receiving facilities.
- **TD-009, the same mechanism at household scale.** Psychiatric framing ("mental health
  challenges") turns a survivor's report into a symptom. Inside an institution, the same
  framing comes with custody, and the referral rate falls below 5%. For a survivor whose
  family has already framed her as mentally ill and refusing treatment, the expanding
  commitment regime turns a credibility problem into a **potential custody risk**. That
  links the history file, the Cicero map, and her case. **It is potential, not documented
  for her.**

## BOUNDARY

**Establishes:**
- documented scale and low referral and prosecution rates, at the stated grades;
- ten accountability-defeating mechanisms, each tied to evidence or marked (gen.);
- the multi-directional nature of the abuse.

**Does NOT establish:**
- national incidence rates (there is no comprehensive US dataset; the data here are case
  series and freedom-of-information counts);
- the truth of any individual allegation;
- that any specific facility or person named is guilty beyond settled or official findings;
